\begin{abstract}
Looped transformers reuse one block of layers many times and are often trained by backpropagating through only the last $k$ loops. We measure how much of the weight gradient this discards, exactly and for every $k$ from one backward pass, on nine looped language models. The share is large. On published models with 16 or 32 loops, keeping one loop discards 80--90\% of a sequence's gradient, and keeping four still discards 42--62\%. Once the loop count is well above $k$ this barely depends on it (within about 0.03 from 32 to 64 loops, a pre-registered check). $\rho^{k}$, with $\rho$ the backward signal's fitted decay per loop, summarises it at moderate loop counts but overstates it at long ones. On Ouro the signal grows instead, and keeping two of four loops discards 81\% of the output's gradient. Yet the size of this number is a poor guide to what truncation costs in training. In fine-tuning, keeping 4 of 32 loops on Parcae-140m discarded 44\% of the gradient and cost 2\% of what training the loop gained; keeping one discarded 82\% and cost 22\%. One reason is that the trained model's number is not what training sees: 300 fine-tuning steps lowered it by 0.07--0.22 on three of four models, on held-out problems too. Another is that the optimiser ignores part of what is lost: on Parcae-140m under AdamW, the full gradient shrunk to the truncated gradient's length cost almost nothing, while the truncated direction at full length cost as much as truncation, as Adam's insensitivity to the gradient's scale predicts. Under SGD the split was mixed: the direction still carried most of the cost, and whether the lost size costs anything there is unclear. In pre-registered tests the lost gradient did not reliably predict the training cost of truncation better than rules that need no measurement. It shows what a given $k$ discards on a checkpoint, and whether the backward signal grows, but to choose $k$ the cost has to be measured.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:1}

Looped transformers reuse one block of layers many times in a row instead of stacking many different blocks. Examples are Huginn (\citealp{geiping2025}), Parcae (\citealp{prairie2026parcae}) and Ouro (\citealp{zhu2025ouro}). Running the block more times lets the model compute longer without adding parameters.

Backpropagating through every loop means storing activations for every loop. So looped models are often trained by sending the learning signal back through only the last $k$ of $R$ loops:

\begin{itemize}
\item Huginn samples the number of loops at random and backpropagates through the last 8 (\citealp[Sec.~3.3]{geiping2025});
\item Parcae uses about the last half (\citealp[Sec.~4.2]{prairie2026parcae});
\item the converted models of \citet{mcleish2025} use at most the last 8.
\end{itemize}

The choice is made by hand or by a pretraining ablation, not from a measurement of the gradient. Prior work has measured training cost against $k$ (\citealp[App.~I]{prairie2026parcae}; \citealp{huang2026partii}; \citealp{xiong2026truncate}), what truncation does to pretraining at fixed compute (\citealp{schwethelm2026a}), and the agreement between truncated and full gradients at single settings (\citealp[App.~C.3 and C.8]{huang2026partii}). For recurrent networks unrolled over time, \citet{aicher2019} bound the bias of truncation geometrically and estimate its decay rate from data. For looped models, how much gradient truncation discards at each $k$ has not been measured.

We measure it on nine looped language models (seven published, two our own conversions), and find that its size is a poor guide to the cost of truncation. Truncation discards most of the gradient: on Parcae-140m at 32 loops, keeping 4 loops discards 44\% of a sequence's gradient, and keeping one discards 82\%. Yet when we fine-tuned with truncation, keeping 4 loops cost 2\% of what training the loop gained, and keeping one 22\%, about 0.04 nats. The paper follows that contrast: how large the lost gradient is and what sets it (Section~\ref{sec:3}), how it compares with the training cost (Section~\ref{sec:4}), how it changes once training starts (Section~\ref{sec:5}), and whether it can predict the cost at all (Section~\ref{sec:6}).

We pre-registered twenty-five studies. In the text, an S-number (S1--S25) marks a pre-registered result, and results found only after the fact are labelled as such; Appendix~\ref{app:B} (Table~\ref{tab:4}) keeps the study-by-study record of questions, freeze commits, verdicts, amendments and exclusions. Only the freezes of S3, S8 and S9--S25 were pushed to GitHub before their runs; for the others the order of freeze and run is our report. What we find:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Truncation discards most of the gradient, and the curve is set near the output (Section~\ref{sec:3}).} One full backward pass per held-out sequence gives the lost gradient for every $k$. On published models with 16 or 32 loops, keeping one loop discards 80--90\% of it and keeping four 42--62\%. Once the loop count is well above $k$ it barely changes with the loop count (S10, S14). $\rho^{k}$ came within about 20\% of it at the loop counts of Table~\ref{tab:1}, but the fitted $\rho$ drifts up as loops are added, so $\rho^{k}$ overstates it at long loop counts (S10, S14), and it overstated it by up to 0.22 on the only points at intermediate $\rho$ (blended SmolLM2). On Ouro the backward signal grows, and keeping two of four loops discards 81\% of the final output's gradient, on unseen test text too (S15, S21).
\item \textbf{The training cost is much smaller (Section~\ref{sec:4}).} In 600-step fine-tunes with AdamW, on every model whose loop gained at least 0.08 nats from training, truncation cost at most 0.29 of that gain at $k = 1$, where the lost gradient was 0.36--0.83. On Ouro, whose loop gained only 0.034 nats, truncation took all of it at $k = 1$ (S18); converted to nats after the fact, it cost about what it did on Parcae.
\item \textbf{The trained model's number falls once training starts, and the cost is mostly in the turn, not the shrink (Section~\ref{sec:5}).} It falls during fine-tuning, by 0.07--0.22 within 300 steps on three of four models, on held-out problems too and under truncated training, and most of the fall remains at the end of the run (S12, S13, S15--S17, S20). That accounts for only part of the contrast. Splitting the truncated gradient into its size and its direction on Parcae-140m under AdamW, the direction alone at full length cost as much as truncation at $k = 1$ and 2, and the full gradient at the truncated length cost almost nothing (S22, S23; S22's own verdict was mixed because of one diverged run). Adam's insensitivity to scale predicts this; the test confirms it holds through training. Under SGD the outcome was mixed: the direction alone carried 78\% of truncation's cost at $k = 1$ and 89\% at $k = 2$, the size alone cost no clear amount, and whether size costs under SGD is unclear (S24).
\item \textbf{It did not reliably predict the cost (Section~\ref{sec:6}).} Within one model a cost curve predicted new settings (S5), but a rule found after the fact did nearly as well, and the curve failed on a second model (S6). Across two new models it did no better than Parcae's own cost at the same $k$ (S11).
\end{enumerate}

The cost results come from fine-tunes of already-trained models, compared at equal steps. In pretraining, truncation costs more: \citet{huang2026partii} report +15\% perplexity for a 400M model trained with one loop kept. So we read our results as evidence about what the lost gradient means, not as a claim that truncation is cheap.

Code, pre-registrations, results and the record of when each freeze was pushed are at \url{https://github.com/Aur-Shalev-Merin/lost-gradient-looped-transformers}.

\section{Measuring the lost gradient}\label{sec:2}

\textbf{Notation.} A looped model applies the same block to a hidden state $R$ times and then reads out. Counting loops back from the output ($j = 0$ is the last loop):

\begin{itemize}
\item $\lambda_j$, the backward signal: the gradient of the loss with respect to the hidden state after loop $j$ (the adjoint);
\item $c_j$, loop $j$'s contribution to the gradient of the shared weights: $\lambda_j$ pushed back through that loop's dependence on those weights;
\item $g$, the full gradient, and $g_k$, the gradient when backprop runs through only the last $k$ loops, which drops the contributions of the loops $j \geq k$ (the layers after the loop contribute the same term to both);
\item $D_k = g - g_k$, the sum of the dropped contributions.
\end{itemize}

The lost gradient is the relative size of what truncation drops, for one held-out sequence:

\begin{equation*}\text{lost gradient}=\frac{|g-g_k|}{|g|}.\end{equation*}

It can exceed 1 when the truncated gradient points partly against the full one.

\textbf{Which number.} \citet{aicher2019} define the relative bias of truncated backprop as the same ratio for expected gradients, $|\mathbb{E}\,g - \mathbb{E}\,g_k| / |\mathbb{E}\,g|$. Our tables report the median over held-out sequences of the per-sequence ratio, over all parameters, on the trained model. It needs no batch or optimiser state. The two can differ: on trained Parcae-140m at $k = 1$, the clipped average over 64 sequences loses 0.97 against a per-sequence 0.80, and the unclipped expected gradient loses 100\% at every $k$, because a few sequences with very large gradients dominate it. After 300 fine-tuning steps they agree within 0.05 (Appendix~\ref{app:I}). The text moves the number by up to 0.12, and the parameters measured and the checkpoint move it too. Appendix~\ref{app:L} lists every protocol and where it is used; to compare models, use one protocol throughout.

\textbf{Measuring it exactly.} One backward pass measures the lost gradient for every $k$:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Run the full backward pass once on the trained model with the weights frozen, and record the backward signal $\lambda_j$ at every loop.
\item Push each loop's signal through that loop's own weight Jacobian, one vector-Jacobian product per loop. This gives each contribution $c_j$.
\item Sum the contributions from the far end. That gives $D_k$, and so the lost gradient, at every $k$ (Appendix~\ref{app:A}).
\end{enumerate}

Equivalently, give each loop its own copy of the shared weights; standard autograd then returns every $c_j$ from one backward pass, at the price of storing a gradient per loop. Table~\ref{tab:1} uses one truncated backward pass per $k$, and Appendix~\ref{app:J} the per-loop contributions. On the same sequences the two agree within 0.001 on Parcae (fp32) and within 0.005 on Huginn (bf16).

\textbf{A one-number summary, $\rho$.} From the same pass, $\rho$ is the backward signal's average shrink per loop: the slope of a straight-line fit to $\log|\lambda_j|$ over all $R$ loops, as a factor, with the median taken over held-out sequences. If the signal shrinks by $\rho$ per loop and the loops push the shared weights in broadly similar directions, the dropped contributions are the tail of a geometric series, and

\begin{equation*}\text{lost gradient}\approx\rho^{k}.\end{equation*}

With $\rho = 0.8$ and 4 loops kept, about 41\% of the gradient is lost; with $\rho = 0.35$, about 1.5\%. Appendix~\ref{app:A} gives exact forms for a finite number of loops. Geometric bounds of this kind are known (\citealp[Thm.~1]{aicher2019}; \citealp{liao2018}; \citealp[Prop.~1]{movahedi2026fprm}). \citet[Sec.~4.2]{aicher2019} also estimate the rate from data, from batch-averaged gradient norms over only the farthest 10\% of lags; we fit every loop of each sequence, a choice that matters at long loop counts (Section~\ref{sec:3}). The decay is close to geometric (Figure~\ref{fig:1}): the median $R^{2}$ of the straight-line fit is 0.99 for Huginn and SmolLM2 and 0.85--0.96 for Parcae, whose decay slows after about 8 loops. When the signal grows ($\rho > 1$), only the exact finite-loop forms apply.

\textbf{A dial for $\rho$.} We can slow the fade on purpose by blending each loop's output with its input, a damped iteration: $x \leftarrow (1-a)\,x + a\,F(x)$. This is the iteration \citet{geng2021} use for phantom gradients. For a loop that fully settles, it leaves the settled state, and the gradient you would get with unlimited loops, unchanged; only the per-loop shrink rate moves (Appendix~\ref{app:A}). Our loops have not fully settled, so the model's function changes too, by at most 0.05 nats on test text in the settings we trained (Appendix~\ref{app:A}).

\section{Trained models: most of the gradient is lost, and the curve is set near the output}\label{sec:3}

We measured Huginn-3.5B, Parcae-1.3B, Parcae-370m, Parcae-140m, Ouro-1.4B, and two versions of SmolLM2-135M (\citealp{allal2025smollm2}) that we converted to loop some of their layers, following \citet{mcleish2025}. For conversion B, looping gained nothing over a never-looped control. Two more published looped models, from \citet{huang2026partii}, appear in Appendix~\ref{app:H} and in the fine-tuning and cost tests; we call them the Part II models, after the title of that paper (``Towards Looped Models Done Right, Part II'').

Three results in this section were pre-registered: that the lost gradient carries over across loop counts while $\rho$ drifts (S10, extended in S14), that successive loops' contributions turn near the output (check A of S9; its other checks failed on Parcae or were uninformative, Appendix~\ref{app:B}), and Ouro's growth checks (S15, S21). The rest are measurements reported without a threshold set in advance.

\textbf{The curve carries over across loop counts; $\rho$ does not.} Once $R$ is well above $k$, the lost gradient depends on $k$ and hardly on $R$. From 32 to 64 loops it moved by at most 0.013 at $k \leq  8$ on Parcae-140m and 0.011 on Parcae-370m, a pre-registered check (S10, bar 0.03), and on Parcae-140m it held to 128 loops (S14). Huginn's moved by up to 0.034 from 32 to 48 loops, just over the same bar in a supportive check, and its value at $k = 8$ creeps up with the loop count; Parcae-370m's value at $k = 8$ had moved by 0.032 at 128 loops. The fitted $\rho$ moves more: from 0.81 to 0.91 on Parcae-370m between 16 and 64 loops, and from 0.85 to 0.91 on Huginn between 8 and 64 (Table~\ref{tab:13}). This is what a settled forward pass would give (we did not measure how far it settles): the last $k$ loops see the same states at any loop count, and the far loops added at longer counts contribute little.

\textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:2}} plots every measurement against $\rho^{k}$. At the loop counts of Table~\ref{tab:1} (23 points on six models), the lost gradient lies between 18\% below and 13\% above $\rho^{k}$, with model-specific offsets of that size, while $\rho$ spans 0.35 to 0.88 and the lost gradient 1.5\% to 90\%. That span comes from two clusters: the released models ($\rho$ 0.80--0.88) and our conversions (0.35--0.38), whose loops settle within a few passes. The dial of Section~\ref{sec:2} adds points: blended Parcae-140m ($\rho$ 0.87--0.94) stays within about 0.03 of the line at every $k$, but blended SmolLM2 (A) ($\rho$ 0.62--0.82), the only points between the two clusters, falls 0.10--0.22 below it at $a = 0.3$ and 0.2. At longer loop counts the lost gradient falls up to 61\% below $\rho^{k}$. We set no threshold in advance; Appendix~\ref{app:J} gives the full comparison.

\textbf{Why $\rho^{k}$ lands close, and why it drifts.} Three parts of the account were pre-registered. Near the output, successive loops' contributions point in different directions, and this turning is in the backward signal itself, not in how each loop maps it to the weights (S9's check A, on Parcae-140m, Parcae-370m and Huginn). The fitted $\rho$ follows the far loops, which fade more slowly than the loops that carry the lost gradient, so $\rho^{k}$ overshoots more as the loop count grows: at 64 loops, $\rho^{k}$ at $k = 4$ is 1.34 [1.19, 1.47] and 1.46 [1.14, 1.65] times the lost gradient on Parcae-140m and -370m (bootstrap 95\% intervals; S10, which confirmed the direction of a trend we had already seen at fewer loops). And a model of the signal as a fast near-output mode plus a slow far mode, fitted on 64-loop profiles, predicted $\rho$ at 96 and 128 loops on Parcae-140m before they were measured: 0.891 [0.875, 0.904] and 0.900 [0.875, 0.910], against 0.898 and 0.908 measured (S14). A trend linear in log(loop count), fitted after the fact, lands inside both intervals too. No fitting window gives a $\rho^{k}$ that is accurate across loop counts (Appendix~\ref{app:J}). \citet[Sec.~5.4]{aicher2019} saw a related far-fade effect in recurrent networks and suggested weighting directions by their effect on the parameters, leaving this to future work; the per-loop contributions do that weighting.

\textbf{Where the signal grows: Ouro.} On Ouro the backward signal grows: about fourfold across the last loop and by 1.2--1.4 times per loop before that, for a fitted $\rho$ of 1.88. Ouro normalises its state between loops in its released code (\citealp[App.~D.1]{guo2026}), so the growth could have reflected the state's size rather than its sensitivity. It does not, or not only: weighting each loop's backward signal by the size of its state still gives a fitted growth of 1.66 per loop, and 1.45 on the state before normalisation, and every sequence grows on both (the smallest at 1.41 and 1.16; S15). Keeping two of four loops discards 81\% of the final output's gradient, against 50--71\% if the signal neither grew nor shrank (the two finite-loop forms of Appendix~\ref{app:A} at $\rho = 1$). On 16 GSM8K test problems, which Ouro has apparently not seen, the signal grows as well (1.49 per loop; 1.35 and 1.15 scale-free; S21), and keeping one or two loops discards 0.93 or 0.85. The loss Ouro trains on, a weighted sum of every loop's output loss (without its entropy term), loses slightly more than the final output: 0.94, 0.85 and 0.73 at $k = 1$, 2 and 3 (S15). With only 4 loops the measurement barely constrains $\rho$, and we take one thing from Ouro: its backward signal grows, and truncation discards most of the gradient. Single sequences can grow on other models too (3 of 32 on Parcae-1.3B), so a median can hide them (Appendix~\ref{app:J}).

\section{The lost gradient against the training cost}\label{sec:4}

\textbf{Setup.} We fine-tuned trained looped models on GSM8K question-and-answer text for 600 steps, with batches of 8 $\times$ 512 tokens and AdamW (\citealp{loshchilov2019}), in three kinds of run: backprop through all $R$ loops, through only the last $k$, and a baseline in which the loop gets no gradient (no loop credit, $k = 0$). The training cost of truncation, scored on GSM8K test problems, is the fraction of full backprop's benefit that is lost:

\begin{equation*}\text{cost}=\frac{\text{loss with $k$ loops}-\text{loss with all loops}}{\text{loss with no loop gradient}-\text{loss with all loops}}.\end{equation*}

So 0 means truncation lost nothing, and 1 means it was as bad as not training the loop. The runs being compared see the same data for the same number of steps, so this is the quality lost per training step, not at equal compute. Learning rates were chosen on held-out loss before the scored runs. On Parcae-140m (S2) and both Part II models (S19), tuning each truncated setting's rate separately chose full backprop's rate every time; on SmolLM2 and Ouro the truncated runs reused that rate. Each number averages 5 seeds, 4 on Parcae-140m (Appendix~\ref{app:B} gives the exclusion rule). Parcae-140m ran at 32 loops, four times its mean training loop count of 8; the Part II models and Ouro ran at their training loop counts, with the token embedding frozen (Appendices H and K).

Table~\ref{tab:2} shows the contrast. On every model whose loop gained at least 0.08 nats from training, the lost gradient was far larger than the cost. Keeping 4 of 32 loops on Parcae-140m discarded 44\% of the gradient and cost 2\% of the loop's benefit; keeping one discarded 82\% and cost 22\%. Across settings on Parcae the cost rose steeply with the lost gradient, roughly as its 3.4th power (the curve of Section~\ref{sec:6}, fitted on 25 settings), so discarding half the gradient cost about 3\% of the benefit. Figure~\ref{fig:3} shows every setting we trained on Parcae-140m and SmolLM2 (A). The two quantities are different shares, of a gradient and of a gain in loss, and nothing requires them to be equal. The point is that the size of the first is a poor guide to the size of the second.

After the fact, we note that the lost gradient orders the cost shares: at $k = 1$ and 2 the five models' shares rise in the order of their lost gradients. That ordering is weak evidence. In nats, Ouro falls below Parcae and the fixed-5-loop model. Parcae and the fixed-5-loop model differ by less than the sampling error, two of the five models supplied the curve of Section~\ref{sec:6}, and Ouro's cost carries the caveats below (Appendix~\ref{app:H}). Section~\ref{sec:6} tests whether the lost gradient adds anything to $k$ alone.

Ouro is the exception: truncation took most of the loop's benefit, 1.13 of it at $k = 1$ and 0.65 at $k = 2$ (S18). The pre-registered mapping read that outcome as cost evidence for a very large lost gradient. Converted to nats after the fact, though, truncation cost about what it did on Parcae (0.038 and 0.022, against 0.037 and 0.017), because the loop's benefit was small: 0.034 nats, against 0.08--0.17 on the other models. It may be small because Ouro appears to have seen GSM8K's training split, and a baseline that trains only its output head takes most of the fine-tune's gain (Appendix~\ref{app:K}). We read Ouro's share mainly as a small-denominator effect, but not only that: keeping one loop did worse than not training the loop on 4 of 5 seeds, and the comparison in nats is not like-for-like (Appendix~\ref{app:K}).

The comparison has limits. These are 600-step fine-tunes of models whose loops are already trained, compared at equal steps. In pretraining, truncation costs more (\citealp{huang2026partii}), and \citet[Sec.~5.2]{xiong2026truncate} find that full backprop through fewer loops beats truncation when the loop count can also be chosen. And the lost gradient here is measured on the trained model, before the fine-tuning it is compared with. The next section shows that this matters.

\section{The trained model's number falls once training starts}\label{sec:5}

The trained model's lost gradient is not what training sees: it falls once fine-tuning starts (pre-registered). That accounts for part of the contrast of Section~\ref{sec:4}. A reading of the rest, found after the fact and then tested, follows: under AdamW the cost is carried by how far the truncated gradient turns, not by how much it shrinks (S22, S23); under SGD the turn carried most of it, and whether the shrink costs is unclear (S24, S25).

\textbf{It falls during fine-tuning (S12, S13, S15--S17, S20).} We followed the lost gradient through the full-backprop fine-tunes of Section~\ref{sec:4} on Parcae-140m at 32 loops, SmolLM2 (A) at 8 and the two Part II models at 5, measuring each as trained and after 300 of its 600 steps, and all but SmolLM2 again at step 600 and on unrelated text. The deciding comparisons were fixed in advance; the paired bootstrap intervals were computed after the fact (Appendix~\ref{app:I}).

With full backprop, the per-sequence lost gradient fell within 300 steps by 0.18--0.22 on Parcae-140m, 0.07--0.16 on the Part II models and at most 0.03 on SmolLM2, at $k = 1$, 2 and 4 (measured in S12, whose pre-set comparison was on the averaged update, below). On GSM8K test problems, which no run trains on, the fall was 90--102\% as large, so it is not the model fitting the sequences it saw (S13). It is in the loop's part of the gradient: leaving out the gradient that gets no credit through the loop, $|g - g_k| / |g - g_0|$ with $g_0$ the gradient at $k = 0$, the fall was 0.72--0.80 times the all-parameter one (S15). A fine-tune through only the last 2 loops lowered it too, by 0.19 on Parcae (full backprop: 0.20) and by 0.07 and 0.09 on the Part II models (0.11), so the fall happens under truncated training as well (S15). Most of it remains at the end of the run: by step 600, as the learning rate decayed to its floor, 8--26\% of it had reversed (S17). Two more fine-tune seeds of each Part II model gave the same picture (S20). The averaged update follows the same course: at step 300 the lost share of the clipped update averaged over 64 sequences lay 0.06--0.27 below the trained model's per-sequence median on Parcae and both Part II models (S12); on Parcae-140m at $k = 1$, 0.58 against 0.80.

This reading has limits. On unrelated text (WikiText) the fall was 0.93 times the GSM8K fall on Parcae, but only 0.05 and 0.33 times on the Part II models (S17), so on those two it is mostly specific to the kind of text being trained on. One explanation we cannot rule out is that the fall partly removes a mismatch between the trained model and the fine-tuning text; a study with early checkpoints (S21) failed one of its pre-set validity checks, so it does not decide the question (Appendix~\ref{app:I}). And the fall is not all a change in the loop's weights: a fine-tune that leaves them fixed (no loop credit) and trains only the layers after the loop, and on Parcae the tied token embedding, reproduces about half of the loop-only fall on Parcae and on the sampled-loop-count model, though little of it (0.14) on the fixed-5-loop model (S16).

\textbf{Shrink versus turn (S22, S23).} The lost gradient mixes two things: how much smaller the truncated gradient is (size ratio $r$) and how far its direction is off (cosine); for one batch, $\mathrm{lost}^2 = 1 + r^2 - 2r\cos$. In Table~\ref{tab:6}'s batch-averaged protocol, on Parcae-140m at $k = 4$, the truncated gradient is 0.79 times the full one's size and points nearly the same way (cosine 0.93). Its lost gradient is 0.40 (0.44 per sequence, Table~\ref{tab:2}), of which a pure shrink to 0.79 would already account for 0.21, and the cost was 0.024. At $k = 1$ it is both smaller (0.46) and turned (cosine 0.61), and the cost was 0.22. We noticed after the fact that this suggests a shrink costs less than a turn, and then tested it. On every step of Section~\ref{sec:4}'s recipe on Parcae-140m (AdamW, global clipping at norm 1) we computed both the full and the truncated gradient on the same batch and trained on one of three: the truncated gradient itself; its direction at the full gradient's length (turn only); or the full gradient's direction at the truncated gradient's length (shrink only). Over five seeds the turn alone cost 0.225 of the loop's benefit at $k = 1$ and 0.093 at $k = 2$, against 0.226 and 0.094 for truncation, and the shrink alone cost $-$0.002 at $k = 2$ (S22). At $k = 1$ one shrink-only run diverged: a spike in the full gradient collapsed its fp16 loss scale, and its test loss ended at 20.4. The other four cost at most 0.007, but S22's exclusion rule covered only the full-backprop runs, so that run counted and the pre-set verdict was mixed. A clean-up on seven new seeds, with every run held to the exclusion rule, gave the same picture at $k = 1$: 0.224 for truncation, 0.224 for the turn alone and 0.001 for the shrink alone (S23; no run diverged). At $k = 4$, reported but not scored, the turn alone cost 0.015 against 0.016. Under AdamW, then, the lost size costs nothing and the change of direction carries the cost, so the lost gradient, which counts both, overstates it (Appendix~\ref{app:G}).

This was largely expected. Adam ignores a constant rescale of the gradient, so each arm's update after Adam stays close to that of the gradient whose direction it keeps: the turn-only arm's agreed with full backprop's as much as truncation's did (0.894 against 0.893 at $k = 1$), and the shrink-only arm's at 0.998--1.000. The pre-registration said the shrink arm should cost little by construction. What the test adds is that neither the step-to-step variation of the size nor clipping at norm 1 changed this over 600 steps of training. It is one model under one recipe, and S23 is a clean-up of S22, not an independent test. The shrink-only arm was also the least stable: over both studies, 2 of its 25 runs crossed the exclusion limit, against none of the 50 truncated and turn-only runs, too few to show a difference (p $\approx$ 0.11). Earlier tests built on a related measure, the direction after Adam's rescaling, were uninformative within Parcae (S8) and tied the lost-gradient curve across models (S11, Section~\ref{sec:6}).

\textbf{Under SGD (S24, S25).} Without Adam a shorter gradient is a shorter step, so we repeated the split with SGD (momentum 0.9) and clipping only as a guard against spikes, at norm 5, at a learning rate of 0.1 chosen by a pilot of full backprop and the floor alone. In training, the truncated gradient was 0.70 ($k = 1$) and 0.84 ($k = 2$) of the full gradient's length, so truncation and the shrink-only arm took steps that much shorter. The pre-set labels were mixed. At $k = 2$ the reading held: truncation cost 0.095, the turn alone 0.085 and the shrink alone 0.012 (``turn''). At $k = 1$ the turn alone cost 0.201 against 0.257 for truncation, 0.005 short of the bar for ``turn'', and the shrink alone 0.039 $\pm$ 0.038, inside its margin (``partial''). So under SGD the turn still carried most of the cost (78\% at $k = 1$, 89\% at $k = 2$), and the pre-set test of the size found no clear cost. After the results came in, giving the truncated direction back its full length looked to recover 0.057 $\pm$ 0.018 of the cost at $k = 1$. A pre-registered test of that difference on new seeds (S25) was inconclusive: 0.013 $\pm$ 0.043 on the 8 of 12 seeds that stayed stable (one-sided 90\% bounds $-$0.049 and 0.074), and the turn alone again carried nearly all of truncation's cost (0.186 of 0.199). A control for a plainly shorter step settled nothing (Appendix~\ref{app:G}).

\textbf{What remains.} At step 300 the averaged update on Parcae still discards 58\% of the gradient at $k = 1$, while the cost at that setting is 22\% of the loop's benefit. S22--S25 say that the part of the lost gradient that is only a change of size costs nothing under AdamW and no clear amount under SGD; they do not say how much a given turn costs, so the cost still cannot be read off the measurement. One candidate we did not test: these fine-tunes start from loops that already work, so truncation limits how the loop adapts, not whether it learns at all.

\section{Can the lost gradient predict the cost?}\label{sec:6}

We tested this at three widths, with predictions locked before training: within one model, across models, and at the extreme.

\textbf{Within one model (S1, S5, S6).} On a given model the lost gradient at each $k$ barely depends on the loop count (Section~\ref{sec:3}). So without an intervention, a rule using the lost gradient and a rule using $k$ alone make the same predictions, and our first study found exactly that (S1). To separate them we used the dial of Section~\ref{sec:2} to change the lost gradient at a fixed $k$.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{S5, Parcae-140m (Figure~\ref{fig:4}, left).} A power law fitted on 25 earlier settings, $\mathrm{cost} = 0.339\cdot(\text{lost gradient})^{3.38}$, predicted four new blended settings with an average error of 0.005, against 0.037 for ``$k$ alone''. That rival was weak, since it ignores the dial, and a rule based on $a\cdot k$ (``how far back the gradient effectively reaches''), which needs no measurement, did nearly as well (0.006 to 0.014, found after the test). S5's freeze has no external timestamp (Appendix~\ref{app:B}).
\item \textbf{S6, SmolLM2 converted (A) (Figure~\ref{fig:4}, right).} The same curve, refitted with S5's results and frozen, predicted that raising the lost gradient at $k = 1$ from 0.36 to 0.65 by blending would cost 5--8\% of the loop's benefit. The cost stayed at most about 1\%, and this model's own unblended cost did better (average error 0.003 against 0.032). At $k = 1$ the dial mostly shrinks the truncated gradient instead of turning it (Appendix~\ref{app:E}), so S6 mainly tested a shrink, which cost little. That observation is where the test of Section~\ref{sec:5} (S22, S23) started; S6 is not independent support for its result.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Across models (S11).} Different models do pay different costs at the same $k$ (Table~\ref{tab:2}), and a measurement of the loop could explain that. Parcae and SmolLM2 cannot test it, since any measure of how fast the loop fades predicts near-zero cost on SmolLM2 (Figure~\ref{fig:3}). We ran the test on the two Part II models of \citet{huang2026partii} (about 304M parameters counting embeddings; 5 loops; Appendix~\ref{app:H}), with four predictions locked before training. Two use the frozen models' measurements and curves fitted on Parcae before these models were measured: the lost-gradient curve of S6, and a curve in the direction of the update after Adam's rescaling. Two need no measurement: Parcae's own cost at the same $k$ (``$k$ alone''), and SmolLM2's. The lost-gradient curve did no better than Parcae's cost: average error 0.037 against 0.036 over $k = 1$, 2 and 4 on both models (Table~\ref{tab:9}). The direction-after-Adam curve's error was 0.051, a tie with the lost-gradient curve by the pre-set rule (neither had half the other's error), and SmolLM2's costs were far off (0.116). The test had little power: a win needed the curve's average error to be half that of ``$k$ alone'', and a seed bootstrap, computed after the fact, puts the difference at $-$0.0015 to +0.0026 (Appendix~\ref{app:H}). After the fact: at the same $k$ the fixed-5-loop model cost 1.5--2.1 times as much as the other, which no rule based on $k$ alone can produce. Both measured curves ordered the two models correctly, but the lost-gradient curve under-predicted the fixed-5-loop model's costs at $k = 1$ and 2 by about a third. Matching the share of loops kept instead of $k$ would have done worse (Appendix~\ref{app:H}).

\textbf{At the extreme: Ouro (S18).} Ouro-1.4B loses the most gradient of any model we measured, so we trained it under a separate pre-registration (Appendix~\ref{app:K}). The locked predictions at $k = 1$ and 2 were 0.26 and 0.19 from the lost-gradient curve (extrapolating at $k = 1$) and 0.22 and 0.10 from Parcae's cost. The cost was 1.13 [1.01, 1.25] and 0.65 [0.51, 0.79] (90\% intervals over 5 seeds; Table~\ref{tab:14}), above both predictions, an outcome we had given 6\% at the freeze. At full backprop's learning rate, keeping one of four loops was no better than not training the loop. The pre-set mapping read this outcome as cost evidence for the lost gradient at the extreme. We read it more weakly, because converted to nats after the fact it is about Parcae's cost (Section~\ref{sec:4}; Appendix~\ref{app:B}). It is also one model, its truncated arms reused full backprop's learning rate, untested on Ouro, and because Ouro appears to have seen GSM8K's training split, its learning rates were chosen on test loss (Appendix~\ref{app:K}).

In these tests the lost gradient did not predict the costs better than $k$ alone. That it orders the cost shares of five models is an observation made after the fact (Section~\ref{sec:4}).

\section{Related work}\label{sec:7}

\textbf{Truncated backprop.} Truncated backpropagation through time is standard for recurrent networks. \citet{pascanu2013} bound the gradient through a recurrence by products of Jacobian norms, so it shrinks or grows geometrically with distance; our backward signal and its fitted $\rho$ are that quantity in a looped transformer. \citet{aicher2019} bound the bias of truncation geometrically (Thm. 1), estimate the decay rate from late lags (Sec. 4.2), note that a few slowly decaying directions inflate the estimated rate (Sec. 5.4), and price relative bias in SGD (Thm. 2). \citet{tallec2017} remove the bias by randomising the truncation length, at the price of extra variance, and \citet{ajalloeian2020} analyse SGD with relative gradient bias. In meta-optimisation, \citet{wu2018shorthorizon} show that a short unroll horizon biases the result toward what is good over that horizon. In small recursive reasoning models truncation can even help: on ARC-AGI, leaving the first 2--3 of 8 loops without gradient raised pass@1 (\citealp[Table 3]{gao2025urm}), and on algorithmic tasks an intermediate gradient horizon generalised best (\citealp[Table 8]{shakhvalieva2026}).

\textbf{Fixed-point models.} Deep equilibrium models (\citealp{bai2019deq}) avoid truncation by differentiating through the fixed point implicitly. For implicit and equilibrium models, a short or one-step gradient often trains well when the loop is contractive (\citealp{fung2022}; \citealp{geng2021}). \citet[Prop.~3]{liao2018} and \citet[Prop.~1]{movahedi2026fprm} bound the truncation error geometrically; Movahedi et al. state it for looped transformers but do not measure it, and their Theorem 3 (damping removes oscillation around the fixed point) underlies our Appendix~\ref{app:C}. In bilevel optimisation, \citet{shaban2019} bound the error of truncated backprop by the slowest mode, and \citet{vicol2022} note that truncation diminishes the weight on slow, low-curvature directions. Our dial is the damped iteration behind \citeauthor{geng2021}'s phantom gradients.

\textbf{Looped language models.} Universal Transformers (\citealp{dehghani2019ut}) apply one weight-shared block repeatedly in depth, and \citet{saunshi2025looped} show that a block looped many times can nearly match a deeper unshared model on reasoning tasks. Huginn (\citealp{geiping2025}) chooses its backprop depth by hand, and Parcae (\citealp{prairie2026parcae}) by a pretraining ablation that reports training cost against $k$ (its App. I); Ouro (\citealp{zhu2025ouro}) states none and apparently backpropagates through all of its loops. \citet{mcleish2025} convert pretrained models into looped ones.

\begin{itemize}
\item HRM-Text (\citealp[App.~C.1]{wang2026hrm}) reports that backpropagating through more loops amplifies the product of loop Jacobians and brings rare, very large gradient spikes, which fits the growing backward signal we see in Ouro.
\item \citet{bansal2022} run a random number of iterations without gradient and then backpropagate through a random number of further ones, which with input re-injection lets recurrent networks run for many more iterations at test time.
\item \citet{schwethelm2026a} pretrain 116 models at equal total depth. Backpropagating through the last half of the loops saves about 30\% of the training compute per token; at fixed compute the extra tokens lower validation loss, but each loop is worth less, which they suggest likely reflects early loops no longer receiving an accurate learning signal.
\item \citet{huang2026partii} pretrain looped models from scratch and report training cost against the backprop window (a 400M model: +15\% perplexity with one loop, within 1\% from four), the agreement between truncated and full gradients at two single settings (cosine 0.978 for the last 10 of 16 loops), and that, with the input re-injected at every loop, truncated training makes the loop settle in the forward pass (their App. C.3).
\item \citet{xiong2026truncate} fit a scaling law over nearly 600 pretraining runs that vary the loop count and the backprop window separately: at a fixed loop count truncation saves compute, but when both are chosen, full backprop through fewer loops is best, and the effective-capacity gain in their fit depends far more on the backprop window than on the loop count (fitted exponents 1.02 and 0.24).
\item \citet{yang2026stars} stabilise Ouro's loop past its training depth with a Jacobian penalty; \citet{guo2026} score a partial forward step $H + \alpha(F(H) - H)$ on frozen Ouro and Huginn, which at $\alpha = 0.25$ is our dial applied once. \citet{shalevmerin2026present} probes Ouro's hidden states for unwritten intermediate results of arithmetic problems and finds a small readable signal that, where tested, is not used for the answer.
\end{itemize}

What we add: exact measurements of the lost gradient on nine looped language models and of how fine-tuning changes it; the finding that the curve barely changes with the loop count while the fitted rate drifts (an effect known for recurrent networks, measured here in pre-registered tests); and the contrast between how much gradient truncation discards and how much training it costs, with pre-registered tests showing that the first did not predict the second better than $k$ alone.

\section{Limitations and takeaways}\label{sec:8}

\textbf{Limitations.} The cost evidence is narrow: 600-step fine-tunes of trained models on one task, compared at equal steps, with AdamW except in one SGD study. No test separated the lost gradient from a rule that needs no measurement (S1, S5, S6, S11). S11 used one pair of small models at 5 loops with the token embedding frozen, so a cost signal across a wider range of models is not ruled out, and S11's ``$k$ alone'' rule took Parcae's cost at the same $k$ (of 32 loops) for models run at 5 loops. Ouro's result is one model, with learning rates chosen on test loss, untuned truncated arms and, at $k = 1$, an extrapolated curve (Appendix~\ref{app:K}). The shrink-versus-turn reading of Section~\ref{sec:5} was found after the fact and then tested on one model, at $k = 1$ and 2, under AdamW with clipping (S22, S23). S22's own verdict was mixed because of one diverged run, S23 is a clean-up rather than an independent test, and the outcome is largely what Adam's insensitivity to scale predicts. Under SGD on the same model (S24) the outcome was mixed, and whether the lost size costs anything there is unclear; its control for a plainly shorter step settled nothing either.

The scope is narrow in other ways. $\rho$ needs a trained model, so it is no help when choosing $k$ for pretraining unless it is tracked during training, which we did not do. The fine-tuning studies follow one full-backprop run, one truncated run ($k = 2$) and one run with no loop credit per model. The Part II models have three seeds throughout (S20), Parcae a second seed only for S13 and S16, and other $k$ and later stages of truncated runs are unmeasured. Every model in Table~\ref{tab:1} with $\rho < 1$ was trained with truncated backprop, and the one apparently trained through all its loops (Ouro) has $\rho > 1$. But the two Part II models, trained through nearly all or all of their loops, have $\rho$ of 0.80 and 0.85, and Ouro differs from every model whose signal shrinks in other ways that may matter more: it does not re-inject the input at each loop, it trains on a loss at every loop, and it normalises its state between loops (Appendix~\ref{app:J}). We tested none of these as causes. The account of why $\rho^{k}$ lands close rests on three frozen models and, beyond S9's check A, S10 and S14, re-describes the data (Appendix~\ref{app:J}). SmolLM2's loops are short: conversion A's settles in about two passes.

On measurement, the lost gradient moves with the text by up to 0.12 (Appendix~\ref{app:F}), and the starred rows of Table~\ref{tab:1} come from a different probe. Parcae and Huginn start the loop from a random state; across 8 redraws, the median within-sequence standard deviation was at most 0.0004 on the three models checked (S17), and we did not check Huginn. An earlier study (not reported here) was scored and then invalidated by a precision bug that silently cut the gradient to the looped weights; every later run checks its truncated gradient in the training precision (Appendix~\ref{app:F}).

\textbf{Takeaways.}

\begin{itemize}
\item Measuring the lost gradient is cheap. One full backward pass per held-out sequence on a trained model gives it for every $k$ (for 32 sequences of Huginn-3.5B on one T4, 52 minutes at 8 loops and 2.6 hours at 64, including the truncated comparisons). Once the loop count is well above $k$, the curve measured at one loop count carries over to longer ones. Compare models only under one protocol.
\item Use it to see what a given $k$ throws away on a checkpoint. Where the backward signal grows, as on Ouro, truncation discards most of the gradient, and a median can hide single sequences that grow on other models. $\rho$ flags growth but is a summary, not a shortcut: it needs the same backward pass and drifts with the loop count.
\item Do not read it as a cost. In our fine-tunes with AdamW the lost gradient was far larger than the share of training lost, and the trained model's value falls once training starts on the kind of text being trained on. To choose $k$, measure the cost with your own model and optimiser. \citet{li2026policymemory} reach the same conclusion for memory gradients in policies trained through a differentiable simulator: gradient norms measured on trained policies did not predict which cut cost more, and short training runs did.
\item Under Adam, which part of the gradient is lost matters more than how much. On Parcae-140m under AdamW with clipping, the truncated gradient's change of direction carried all of truncation's cost and its loss of size carried none (S22, S23), as Adam's insensitivity to scale predicts. That is one model. Under SGD the direction still carried most of the cost, and whether the loss of size costs anything there is unclear (S24). A cost curve built on a related measure did not beat $k$ alone on the Part II models (S11).
\end{itemize}

\section{The rule}\label{app:A}

Write the full gradient over the loop's parameters as $g = \sum_j c_j$, where $c_j$ is the contribution of the loop $j$ steps before the output, and keeping $k$ loops gives $g_k = \sum_{j<k} c_j$. Over all parameters, the layers after the loop add the same term to both, while the layers before it get gradient only through the loops. If the backward signal shrinks by $\rho$ per loop, $|c_j| \propto \rho^{j}$.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Aligned contributions:} lost gradient = $(\rho^{k}-\rho^{R})/(1-\rho^{R})$.
\item \textbf{Mutually orthogonal contributions:} $\sqrt{(\rho^{2k}-\rho^{2R})/(1-\rho^{2R})}$.
\item \textbf{In between:} both forms are $\approx$ $\rho^{k}$ for $\rho < 1$ and $R \gg  k$. When no two contributions point against each other, the decay rate is exactly $\rho$ and only a constant factor is uncertain: for $R \gg  k$ the lost gradient lies within a factor $\sqrt{(1+\rho)/(1-\rho)}$ of $\rho^{k}$ either way, about 3.5 at $\rho = 0.85$. Proof: write $s_j = |c_j|$, $S_k = \sum_{j\geq k} s_j$ and $Q_k = \sum_{j\geq k} s_j^2$. With no negative inner products, any set A of loops has $\sum_A s_j^2 \leq |\sum_A c_j|^2 \leq (\sum_A s_j)^2$. Since $|D_0|^2 \geq |D_k|^2 + (Q_0 - Q_k)$ and $|D_k| \leq S_k$, the lost gradient is at most $S_k/\sqrt{S_k^2 + Q_0 - Q_k}$; since $|D_0| \leq |D_k| + (S_0 - S_k)$ and $|D_k| \geq \sqrt{Q_k}$, it is at least $\sqrt{Q_k}/(\sqrt{Q_k} + S_0 - S_k)$. Both bounds are sharp: the dropped contributions all parallel and the kept ones orthogonal to everything attain the upper one, and the dropped contributions mutually orthogonal with every kept one parallel to their sum attain the lower one. With $s_j = \rho^{j}$ and $R \to \infty$ their ratio to $\rho^{k}$ tends to the factor above as $k$ grows. At $\rho = 0.85$ and $k = 4$ they are 0.24 and 0.91, against $\rho^{4} = 0.52$.
\item \textbf{Measured-profile forms:} when the signal size $s_j$ is measured loop by loop instead of assumed geometric, the aligned and orthogonal forms become $\sum_{j\geq k} s_j / \sum_j s_j$ and $\sqrt{\sum_{j\geq k} s_j^2 / \sum_j s_j^2}$. Appendix~\ref{app:J} compares them with the measured lost gradient. In all-parameter measurements the gradient of the layers after the loop also enters the denominator. Huginn and Parcae-1.3B are measured over loop parameters only, where it is absent, and they lie above both forms too.
\item \textbf{Uneven angles:} the two forms assume every pair of contributions meets at the same angle. When the angles differ, the lost gradient can lie outside both forms with no cancellation at all. Call the coherence of a set of contributions the size of their sum divided by the sum of their sizes. Then, exactly, the lost gradient equals the aligned form on the measured sizes, $\sum_{j\geq k} |c_j| \,/\, \sum_j |c_j|$, times the coherence of the dropped loops divided by that of all loops. It lies above that form exactly when the dropped loops add up more coherently than all loops together: when they line up with each other more than the kept ones do, or when the kept and the dropped loops point in different directions. It lies below it in the reverse case. Without cancellation, the lost gradient lies between the sharp bounds of the first bullet, computed on the measured sizes, a much wider range than the two forms. The cruder bounds $\sqrt{\sum_{j\geq k} |c_j|^2} \,/\, \sum_j |c_j|$ and $\sum_{j\geq k} |c_j| \,/\, \sqrt{\sum_j |c_j|^2}$ (with the signal sizes standing in for |$c_j$|) give 0.17--0.91 for Huginn at $R = 8$, $k = 4$, and 0.16--0.80 for blended SmolLM2 (A) at $a = 0.2$; the sharp ones are narrower. For example, kept loops all pointing one way and dropped loops pointing in unrelated directions give about 0.23 for the latter, its measured value. So a point outside both forms does not by itself show cancellation.
\item \textbf{Tail sums:} write $D_k$ for the sum of the contributions of loops $k$ and beyond, so that over the loop's parameters the lost gradient is $|D_k| / |D_0|$, and each contribution is the difference of two successive sums, $c_k = D_k - D_{k+1}$. For a loop that has settled, the tail sums are the remainder terms of a Neumann series (\citealp[App., eq.~9]{liao2018}). If the sum shrinks by a factor r per loop while turning by an angle $\theta_k$, then $|c_k| = |D_k|\sqrt{1 + r^2 - 2r\cos\theta_k}$. Turning inflates the per-loop sizes without changing what is lost. Each contribution can be computed exactly from one backward pass: loop k's recorded backward signal pushed through that loop's weight Jacobian, as in Section~\ref{sec:2}.
\item \textbf{When the rule is exact.} Write each contribution as $c_j = \rho^{j} b_j$, with the geometric shrink taken out. Then $D_k = \rho^{k} \sum_{i<R-k} \rho^{i} b_{i+k}$ and $D_0 = \sum_{i<R} \rho^{i} b_i$. Two cases make the lost gradient $\rho^{k}$ for $R \gg  k$ (in the second case its root-mean-square version), however much the contributions turn.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A fixed rotation:} if $b_{j+1} = Q b_j$ for an orthogonal Q, the first sum is $Q^{k}$ applied to the second with its last $k$ terms removed. Both sums tend to the same size as $R$ grows, so the lost gradient tends to $\rho^{k}$ in every sequence.
\item \textbf{Stationary statistics:} if the $b_j$ are random and the expected inner product of $b_i$ and $b_j$ depends only on the lag j $-$ i, the expected squared size of $\sum_{i<N} \rho^{i} b_{i+k}$ is $\sum_{i,i'<N} \rho^{i+i'} C(i'-i)$, with C the expected inner product at each lag, which does not depend on $k$. So $\mathbb{E}|D_k|^2 / \mathbb{E}|D_0|^2$ tends to $\rho^{2k}$, with corrections of order $\rho^{R-k}$. This holds for the ratio of expectations, not for each sequence or for the median over sequences that we report.
\end{itemize}
\item \textbf{$\rho > 1$:} both finite-R forms still hold. For Ouro (median $\rho = 1.88$, $R = 4$) the medians over sequences of each sequence's forms are 0.92 (aligned) and 0.99 (orthogonal) at $k = 1$ and 0.78 and 0.96 at $k = 2$.
\end{itemize}

Blending, $x \leftarrow (1-a)\,x + a\,F(x)$, maps each eigenvalue $\mu$ of the loop's Jacobian to $1 - a + a\mu$. It leaves each mode's weight, and the unlimited-loop (implicit) gradient, unchanged. Over-blending ($a > 1$) can push negative eigenvalues towards $-$1. Such a mode can then set the decay rate of the backward signal, and so $\rho$, while carrying little weight in the gradient (its weight is divided by 1 + |$\mu$|), and its alternating terms partly cancel in the first-order cost of truncation. We infer that this is what happens in Appendix~\ref{app:C}; we did not measure the per-loop contributions.

At the finite loop counts we use, the loop has not fully settled, so blending changes the model's function too. The change in the trained model's loss, before any fine-tuning:

\begin{itemize}
\item Parcae-140m at 64 loops ($a = 0.3$): +0.001 on validation text, +0.02 on test text;
\item Parcae-140m at 48 loops ($a = 0.4$): +0.002 on validation text, +0.05 on test text;
\item SmolLM2 at $a = 0.5$ and 0.3: at most +0.007 on both; at $a = 0.2$, measured but never trained: +0.04.
\end{itemize}

The aligned form is the ratio of two geometric sums. The orthogonal form follows because mutually orthogonal contributions satisfy $|\sum c_j|^2 = \sum |c_j|^2$. The bounds are proved above. All formulas are checked numerically on small linear loops (\texttt{code/law1\_toy\_check.py} in the code release); further results (mixtures of modes, damping) are in the supplementary material (\texttt{results\_docs/law1\_derivation\_formal.md} in the code release).

\section{Overshooting loops}\label{app:C}

On Parcae-140m, over-blending raises the measured $\rho$ instead of lowering it:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{$a = 1.15$:} $\rho = 0.885$, and the lost gradient at $k = 4$ is 0.40, against $\rho^{4} = 0.61$.
\item \textbf{$a = 1.3$:} $\rho = 0.944$, and the model's loss also changes by 0.055.
\end{itemize}

The backward signal decays slowly in size, yet the lost gradient falls faster than $\rho^{k}$. Appendix~\ref{app:A} gives the likely reason, inferred rather than measured: over-blending makes a slow, sign-alternating mode set $\rho$, while that mode carries little of the gradient.

\textbf{Why blending at $k = 1$ mostly shrinks the gradient.} For a settled loop, at $k = 1$ the dial changes only the size of the truncated gradient, not its direction. Near a settled state with loop Jacobian $J$, let $b$ be the last loop's direct contribution to the weight gradient. With blending $a$, the last loop contributes $a\cdot b$, while the full gradient does not depend on $a$ (Appendix~\ref{app:A}). So keeping one loop gives a smaller gradient pointing the same way: a times smaller for a loop that has fully settled, with unlimited loops. In Table~\ref{tab:7} the size ratio falls less than that, to 0.52--0.61 of its unblended value at $a = 0.3$--0.5, on both models. Our loops are finite and not exactly settled, and we did not measure the truncated and full gradients' sizes separately, so we cannot say which of the two departs from the derivation. With $k \geq  2$, blending also reweights the earlier loops against the last one, which can turn the gradient: at $k = 2$ and 4 on Parcae it turned the direction after Adam and the cost rose, while on SmolLM2 at $k = 2$ it barely did (0.998 to 0.988) and the cost rose only slightly (from $-$0.001 to 0.004, against 0.021 predicted at $a = 0.3$). This may also explain why blending did not raise Parcae's cost at $k = 1$ in an earlier study (S2). So every blended setting in S6 was one where the dial mainly shrank the gradient, and its outcome was largely built into the design. S5's settings kept 4 to 12 loops on Parcae, where blending does turn the gradient. When blending turns the gradient differs between the models; we have no simple rule for it.

The derivation holds only approximately here. SmolLM2's raw cosine at $k = 1$ does fall as blending increases (0.935, 0.917, 0.869 in Table~\ref{tab:6}), probably because with 8 loops at $a = 0.3$ the early loops are far from the settled state and the ``full'' gradient itself covers only 8 loops. Why the direction after Adam's rescaling stays flat while the raw direction falls, we cannot explain. Clipping does not remove the size difference: frozen-model gradients were 2--9 times the clip limit, and during fine-tuning the full gradient stayed above the limit on 99--100\% of steps (S12, Appendix~\ref{app:I}). The truncated gradient is smaller, though: at $k = 1$ and 2 it often falls below the limit, so the $k = 1$ and $k = 2$ runs were clipped on only 14\% and 30\% of steps in the S8 pilot on Parcae-140m (Appendix~\ref{app:G}), and clipping then shrinks the full gradient but not the truncated one. Whether the loop has settled does not separate the models either: both settle (SmolLM2 in about two passes; Parcae by $R = 32$, where truncation is still costly).

\textbf{The direction after Adam, a lead.} It ignores size by construction. It is 0.97--1.00 on SmolLM2 in every setting against 0.79--0.94 on Parcae (Tables~\ref{tab:6} and~\ref{tab:7}), and a curve in it fitted on Parcae predicted SmolLM2's costs (D2 below). The raw direction, before Adam, does not order the models: Parcae at a raw cosine of 0.93 costs 2.4\%, SmolLM2 at 0.87 costs 0.9\%. We found this measure only after trying several, could not test it on Parcae-370m (D4), and an attempt to test it within Parcae was uninformative (Appendix~\ref{app:G}). A pre-registered test on two further models (S11; Section~\ref{sec:6}, Appendix~\ref{app:H}) did not support it: its curve over-predicted their costs at $k = 1$ and 2 by 32--59\% and did worse than Parcae's own cost at the same $k$.

\textbf{Other attempts.} All were measured on frozen models, fitted on the Parcae-140m costs, and scored on SmolLM2 converted (A).

\section{Checks and bugs}\label{app:F}

\textbf{Precision bug (an earlier study).} Under PyTorch automatic mixed precision, the loops run without gradient were computed first. They cached low-precision copies of the shared weights with no gradient connection. The later loops reused those copies, so the looped weights received almost no gradient: the truncated gradient was 87\% wrong.

The study had been scored before this was found, and it was invalidated. Every later job checks, on the GPU before training:

\begin{itemize}
\item the truncated gradient in the training precision against full precision;
\item that the written-out forward pass matches the model's own;
\item that gradient checkpointing changes nothing.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Averaging bug (D2's first run).} Averaging raw gradients over batches let rare batches with huge gradients (norm up to 24,865 against a typical 7) dominate. Training clips any step whose gradient exceeds the limit, so the fix was to clip each batch before averaging. A sanity check flagged the problem before the run was scored.

\textbf{Long sums in float32 (an earlier version of Table~\ref{tab:6}).} Cosines summed in float32 over about $1.4\times 10^{8}$ weights on CPU ran high by 0.004--0.007, and one after-Adam cosine reached an impossible 1.017. Table~\ref{tab:6} was recomputed with all sums in float64. The after-Adam measure of D2 already used float64.

\textbf{Checking the starred rows of Table~\ref{tab:1}.} On Parcae-140m and SmolLM2 (A), from the same backward passes, we measured the lost gradient over all parameters, over the starred rows' part of the loop (input-injection layer plus first loop layer), over the whole loop, and over each loop layer alone. The part used for Huginn and Parcae-1.3B agrees with all parameters to within 0.025 at every $k$ on both models (Parcae-140m 0.82 / 0.65 / 0.40 against 0.81 / 0.66 / 0.42; SmolLM2 0.38 / 0.15 / 0.018 against 0.36 / 0.14 / 0.016, all on GSM8K validation text). A single loop layer is less reliable: across SmolLM2's eight loop layers it ranges from 0.22 to 0.41 at $k = 1$, against 0.36 over all parameters. On Ouro, a single middle loop layer reads 0.87 / 0.68 on Table~\ref{tab:1}'s sequences, below the all-parameter 0.92 / 0.81. Low precision does not matter: bfloat16, used for Huginn, moves the part-of-loop number by at most 0.015 on Parcae-140m.

The starred rows also come from a separate probe (32 texts of 128 tokens, half WikiText and half GSM8K, with its own loop code). Run on Parcae-140m in that setting, it reads 0.83 / 0.67 / 0.45 at $k = 1 / 2 / 4$, against 0.81 / 0.66 / 0.42 in Table~\ref{tab:1}, and its own $\rho$ (0.82) predicts 0.82 / 0.67 / 0.45. Given identical sequences, the two scripts give identical numbers. What does move the number is the text: the probe's WikiText and GSM8K halves differ by up to 0.12 at $k = 4$, and a median over 16--32 sequences has a sampling error of about 0.02--0.05.

\textbf{Unstable runs.} Low-precision full-backprop runs occasionally became unstable. A seed is excluded if its full-backprop run has more than 10 numerically unstable steps. The rule was fixed before it was applied.

\section{An attempted test of the direction-after-Adam lead (S8, uninformative)}\label{app:G}

\textbf{Design.} On Parcae-140m we mixed the full gradient with a $k = 1$ or a $k = 4$ truncated gradient, $g = g_\mathrm{full} + \lambda(g_k - g_\mathrm{full})$, rescaled to the full gradient's size. Five pairs of a $k = 1$ and a $k = 4$ arm were matched on the in-training agreement of Adam's update directions with a full-backprop shadow optimiser ($\bar{A}$), using one pilot seed. The pre-registered question: at matched $\bar{A}$, do the two arms cost the same (equivalence test, margin $\max(0.015,\ 0.25\times\mathrm{cost})$, adjusted for residual $\bar{A}$ differences)? 12 arms $\times$ 8 evaluation seeds, plus unscored contrasts and a pipeline check (109 runs), 600-step fine-tunes as in Section~\ref{sec:4}.

\textbf{Result: uninformative.} On the 8 scored seeds the $k = 4$ arms' agreement fell below the pilot's, so three of the five pairs failed the pre-registered drift guard ($|\text{mean}\ \Delta\bar{A}| \leq 0.01$: 0.011, 0.038 and 0.022). Of the two scored pairs, one was ``same'' and one undecided, short of the three scored pairs needed for a verdict. The pilot had matched on a single seed; the $k = 4$ arms' seed-to-seed spread near their unstable dose range was larger than assumed.

\textbf{Descriptive only.} One curve, $\mathrm{cost} = 1.33\cdot(1-\bar{A})^{0.94}$, fits all ten mixed arms of both kinds (average error 0.011). At matched $\bar{A}$, the $k = 1$ arm cost slightly more than the $k = 4$ arm in every pair (by 0.005 to 0.022, 4--31\% of the cost), inside the equivalence margin in every case. Each gap is small, but the same sign in all five pairs hints that $\bar{A}$ misses part of what sets the cost, which matters for any test built on it. An unplanned look at 81 of the 109 runs was taken before the last kernel finished; nothing was changed after it.

\textbf{A direct split of size and direction (S22, S23).} Same model, recipe and pipeline as S8, including its fp16 autocast with manual loss scaling: a step with a non-finite gradient is skipped and the scale halved, and the scale is never raised again. Each step computes the full gradient g\_R and the truncated $g_k$ on the same batch and draw of the initial state, and trains on one of:

\begin{itemize}
\item truncation, g = $g_k$;
\item turn only, g = $g_k$ $\cdot$ |g\_R| / |$g_k$|;
\item shrink only, g = g\_R $\cdot$ |$g_k$| / |g\_R|.
\end{itemize}

Each is clipped to norm 1 and stepped with AdamW at 3e-4, full backprop's rate (in S2 every truncated cell chose it). Full backprop and the no-loop-gradient floor ran through S8's pipeline unchanged. The cost of an arm is (L\_arm $-$ L\_full) / (L\_floor $-$ L\_full) on GSM8K test, paired by seed and averaged over five scored seeds. The pre-set label at each $k$, with margin m = max(0.015, 0.2 $\times$ truncation's cost), was ``turn'' if the turn-only arm's cost was within m of truncation's and the shrink-only arm's was at most m; ``shrink'' if the shrink-only arm's cost was at least half of truncation's; ``partial'' otherwise.

\textbf{S22} ($k = 1$, 2 and 4; verdict decided at $k = 1$ and 2; floor gap 0.166 nats). At $k = 1 / 2 / 4$, truncation cost 0.226 / 0.094 / 0.016, the turn-only arm 0.225 / 0.093 / 0.015, and the shrink-only arm 22.9 / $-$0.002 / $-$0.004 ($k = 4$ not scored). The $k = 1$ shrink-only mean comes from one run. On seed 2391105 the full gradient spiked to a norm of about 291,000 near step 300, the loss scale halved 24 times, and the model collapsed: test loss 20.4 against 1.37 for full backprop, a cost of 114.5. The other four runs cost 0.007, 0.002, $-$0.006 and 0.001. So the labels were ``shrink'' at $k = 1$ and ``turn'' at $k = 2$, and the verdict was mixed. The truncated arm reproduced S2's costs (0.224 / 0.100 / 0.024) within the seed spread at $k = 1$ and 2.

\textbf{S23} ($k = 1$; seven new seeds, all valid, the first five scored; floor gap 0.165 nats): truncation 0.224 $\pm$ 0.006, turn only 0.224 $\pm$ 0.005, shrink only 0.001 $\pm$ 0.001 ($\pm$ one standard error over seeds), margin 0.045: ``turn''. Pooled after the fact over the 12 valid $k = 1$ seeds of both studies: 0.223, 0.222 and 0.002.

\textbf{Agreement after Adam and stability.} The agreement of Adam's update direction with a full-backprop shadow ($\bar{A}$) was the same for truncation and the turn-only arm (0.893 / 0.945 / 0.983 at $k = 1 / 2 / 4$) and 0.998--1.000 for the shrink-only arm, as Adam's invariance to a constant rescale predicts. A report-only amendment to S23, made before any of its output was read, counts unstable runs (more than 10 skipped steps or a non-finite test loss) over all 101 runs of both studies, backups included:

\begin{itemize}
\item shrink only: 2 of 25;
\item truncation: 0 of 25;
\item turn only: 0 of 25;
\item full backprop and floor: 0 of 26.
\end{itemize}

Shrink-only against truncation and turn-only gives p $\approx$ 0.11 (Fisher's exact test). The second unstable run was S22's unscored backup at $k = 4$, with 11 skipped steps and a normal test loss. Mean skipped steps per run were 3.1 for the shrink-only arm (2.2 without the diverged run) and 1.4 for the other two. All three arms compute both gradients on every step, so a spike can reach any of them; they differ only in which update they take.

\textbf{Under SGD (S24).} The same three arms and pipeline, with AdamW replaced by SGD with momentum 0.9 and no weight decay (S7's optimiser), and global clipping at norm 5 in place of 1, so that clipping acts only on spikes (1--5\% of steps). The floor ran S8's pipeline with the same optimiser swap. Learning rates came from a pilot on one unscored seed that trained only full backprop (0.03, 0.1, 0.3) and the floor (1/30, 0.1, 0.3, 1.0), choosing on validation loss among stable runs by a rule pushed before the pilot ran. Both chose 0.1; both diverged at 0.3. The pilot's full-backprop run also measured how much truncation shortens the gradient during training: the clipped truncated gradient was 0.70 ($k = 1$) and 0.84 ($k = 2$) of the clipped full gradient's length (median over steps 100--600). We had assumed about half, from one sequence on the untrained model. Two reported controls, full backprop at learning rates 0.0702 and 0.0836, apply the same shrinks as a constant. The labels and margins are S22's; the validity rule is S23's. Six seeds ran. One was invalid because its full-backprop run diverged, and the other five were scored (floor gap 0.227 nats, 90\% lower bound 0.224).

At $k = 1 / 2$, truncation cost 0.257 $\pm$ 0.012 / 0.095 $\pm$ 0.014, the turn-only arm 0.201 $\pm$ 0.007 / 0.085 $\pm$ 0.012, and the shrink-only arm 0.039 $\pm$ 0.038 / 0.012 $\pm$ 0.020. The margins were 0.052 and 0.019. Labels: ``partial'' at $k = 1$ (the turn-only arm fell 0.005 short of its bar) and ``turn'' at $k = 2$, so the verdict was mixed. The shrink-only arm's $k = 1$ costs ranged from $-$0.070 to 0.167 over seeds. After the fact, paired by seed, truncation minus turn only was 0.057 $\pm$ 0.018 at $k = 1$ and 0.010 $\pm$ 0.020 at $k = 2$. Truncation minus both single arms was 0.018 $\pm$ 0.051 and $-$0.002 $\pm$ 0.018, so the two parts add up to truncation's cost within the noise. The momentum buffer's agreement with a full-backprop shadow was about 0.80 / 0.91 for truncation and the turn-only arm, and 0.997--0.999 for the shrink-only arm.

The step-matched controls cost 0.037 $\pm$ 0.053 ($k = 1$, four seeds) and $-$0.033 $\pm$ 0.013 ($k = 2$). Against them the shrink-only arm read ``as a shorter step'' at $k = 1$ and ``beyond a shorter step'' at $k = 2$, but neither reading carries weight. At $k = 1$ the control's mean comes from one run with 11 skipped steps (cost 0.192); with S23's rule applied to the controls as well, it is $-$0.015 $\pm$ 0.015 over three seeds, and the reading would flip. At $k = 2$ the control is negative: a constant learning rate 16\% lower trained slightly better than 0.1. The pilot's grid was too coarse to show that.

Truncation's own cost was close to S7's under SGD with clipping at norm 1 (0.257 and 0.095 against 0.263 and 0.104; Appendix~\ref{app:D}), though S7's clipping acted on most steps.

Stability reversed from AdamW. None of the 36 two-gradient runs crossed the limit, against 3 of 18 single-gradient runs at learning rates 0.07--0.1 (full backprop 1 of 6, the $k = 1$ control 2 of 6, the $k = 2$ control 0 of 6) and none of the 6 floor runs.

\textbf{Clean-up at $k = 1$ under SGD (S25).} S24's pre-set test of the size, the shrink-only arm, found no clear cost, but after the results came in truncation minus the turn-only arm was 0.057 $\pm$ 0.018 at $k = 1$, paired by seed. The two arms share the truncated direction and differ only in length (0.69 of full backprop's, against full length), so under SGD that difference is the most direct measure available of whether the lost size costs. S25 tested it on twelve new seeds with S24's frozen code and setting: full backprop, the floor, truncation, turn only and shrink only at $k = 1$, with no step-matched controls. The first ten valid seeds were to be scored, with eight the minimum. Simulated with S24's per-seed spread (0.039), the rule called a true D of 0.03 ``costs'' 84\% of the time and a true D of 0 ``free'' 45\% of the time.

Pooled with S24's five seeds, also after the fact, D was 0.030 $\pm$ 0.028 over 13 seeds (bounds $-$0.008 / 0.067); truncation cost 0.221, turn only 0.192 and shrink only 0.003. Over S24 and S25, full backprop at learning rate 0.1 was unstable in 4 of 18 runs, against 2 of 72 two-gradient runs.

\textbf{The fitting window.} The backward signal near the output is the same at every loop count; adding loops only lengthens the stretch over which $\rho$ is fitted. Fitting the first 32 or 48 loops of each 64-loop profile reproduces the $\rho$ of the separate 32- and 48-loop runs (median per-sequence difference at most 0.005; the medians of $\rho$ themselves differ by up to 0.009). This window check, more than S14's prediction, is the evidence for the window account. A $\rho$ fitted only on the 16 loops next to the output does not drift: from 32 to 128 loops it stays within 0.005 of 0.785 on both Parcae models. But its $\rho^{k}$ understates the lost gradient at every loop count, by up to 30\% on Parcae-140m and 41\% on Parcae-370m (computed after the fact from the stored profiles), because near the output the backward signal shrinks faster than the dropped sum does. So no fitting window gives a $\rho^{k}$ that is accurate across loop counts.

\textbf{A cheaper stand-in.} The summed backward signal keeps only a running sum of the backward signal over loops, the size of a hidden state rather than of the parameters. It came within 0.01--0.03 of the loop's lost share on Parcae and Huginn, but was off by up to 0.18 on the two Part II models (Appendix~\ref{app:H}).

\textbf{Ouro on test text (S21, Part B).} S21 measured Ouro on the first 16 GSM8K test chunks, packed as our validation chunks are. Its pre-set rule called growth if the median fitted $\rho$ exceeded 1 for the raw backward signal and for both scale-free versions: 1.49, 1.35 and 1.15 (per-chunk minima 1.31, 1.21 and 0.99, so one chunk does not grow on the pre-norm state). S21's validity checks belong to its Part A, the fine-tunes of Appendix~\ref{app:I}.

\textbf{Where the rule needs care.} With only 4 loops the measurement barely constrains Ouro's $\rho$. We did not record Ouro's exit distribution, so we cannot say how much of its training objective's weight falls on the last loop. Others report that Ouro's loop does not settle in the forward pass either (\citealp[Sec.~1]{huang2026partii}); those results concern the forward pass, and their link to a growing backward signal is only suggestive. Its accuracy falls past four loops (\citealp{yang2026stars}), and reusing one key-value cache slot across loop steps drops its accuracy from 78\% to 0.2\%, while Huginn is unaffected (\citealp[Table 4]{schwethelm2026b}). The warning applies to single sequences too: even at Table~\ref{tab:1}'s loop counts a few sequences have $\rho_i > 1$ (3 of 32 on Parcae-1.3B, up to 1.41; one of 16 on Parcae-140m and on Parcae-370m), and one Parcae-370m sequence loses nearly all of its gradient at every loop count we measured. The rule also fails for a loop that overshoots (Appendix~\ref{app:C}). A loop's largest stretch factor (the largest singular value of its Jacobian) is no guide: about 14 for Huginn and 30 for Parcae-1.3B, whose signals shrink, so a bound built from it says nothing useful here.

\textbf{Why Ouro might differ.} Ouro differs from every model whose signal shrinks in ways that may matter. It does not re-inject the input at each loop (\citealp[Eq.~1]{zhu2025ouro}), so its prompt reaches later loops only through the loop itself, and a loop that shrank everything it passed back would also erase the prompt. This argues against $\rho$ well below 1, though not for $\rho$ as large as 1.88. Ouro also trains on a weighted loss at every loop, so its early loops get gradient from their own outputs (under such a loss a loop's own learning signal scales with its exit probability, and an early collapse of the exit gate can starve later loops (\citealp{marchenko2026})), and it normalises the state between loops. Training through all loops does not by itself make the signal grow: the two Part II models, trained through nearly all or all of their loops, have $\rho$ of 0.80 and 0.85 (Appendix~\ref{app:H}). \citet[App.~C.3]{huang2026partii} show, by comparing truncated and full training at a fixed depth, that truncated training makes a loop that re-injects its input settle in the forward pass (without input injection it does not, which is Ouro's case); our backward-pass pattern is consistent with that but only observed. We tested none of these as causes.

\textbf{The drift as a fitting-window effect (S14).} Write $s_j$ for the size of the backward signal j loops before the output. Near the output it does not depend on the loop count $R$, so the fitted $\rho$($R$) is the slope of one fixed curve, $\log s_j$, over a window that grows with $R$. Take $s_j$ as a fast and a slow mode, $A\mu_f^{\,j} + B\mu_s^{\,j}$. Its log is close to a hockey stick with slope $a_f = \log\mu_f$ up to a crossover $j^*$ and $a_s = \log\mu_s$ after it, and a least-squares line over [0, R], treating j as continuous and with $u = j^*/R \leq 1$ (for $j^* \geq R$ the fit gives $\mu_f$), then has slope

\begin{equation*}\log\rho(R) = a_s + (a_f - a_s)(3u^2 - 2u^3),\qquad u = j^*/R.\end{equation*}

With $x = j/R$ uniform on [0, 1], the slope is $a_s + (a_f - a_s)\,\mathrm{Cov}(x, -(u-x)_+)/\mathrm{Var}(x)$, and $\mathrm{Cov}(x, -(u-x)_+) = u^2/4 - u^3/6$, while $\mathrm{Var}(x) = 1/12$. The fitted rate moves from $\mu_f$ toward $\mu_s$ as $R$ grows, while the lost gradient, which lives near the output, stays put. On the 64-loop profiles the median fits are $\mu_f$ 0.75, $\mu_s$ 0.91 and $j^* \approx 17$ on Parcae-140m (0.71, 0.93 and 10 on Parcae-370m). Applied per sequence, the closed form gives medians of $\rho = 0.830 / 0.866 / 0.876$ at 32 / 48 / 64 loops, against 0.825 / 0.852 / 0.871 measured; these come from the same data and only show that the form fits. The test was S14, pre-registered: the exact least-squares slope of each sequence's fitted two-mode curve (the frozen predictor; the closed form gives nearly the same, 0.894 and 0.902) predicted $\rho$ at 96 and 128 loops on Parcae-140m as 0.891 [0.875, 0.904] and 0.900 [0.875, 0.910], measured 0.898 and 0.908, with the paired rise from 64 to 128 loops +0.039 [+0.009, +0.047] and the lost gradient unchanged. Both measured values sit about 0.008 above the predicted centre, so the two-mode fit slightly understates how far the fitted rate keeps rising. The test does not single out the two-mode model, though: a trend linear in log(loop count), fitted to the 16--64-loop medians after the fact, gives 0.888 and 0.903, also inside both intervals, while a straight line in the loop count gives 0.921 and 0.969, outside. The window check is what supports the mechanism. Parcae-370m's intervals were too wide to test (they contained its 64-loop value); it rose by +0.025 [+0.013, +0.042]. Code: \texttt{paper\_drift\_derivation.py}, \texttt{v204\_score.py}.

\textbf{The rule is not automatic.} $\rho$ comes from the backward signal through the hidden state, while the lost gradient is a sum of each loop's contribution to the weight gradient. If each contribution scales with the backward signal and none point against each other, the lost gradient shrinks at exactly the rate $\rho$, but its level can still differ from $\rho^{k}$ by a sizeable factor (the proven bounds allow a factor of about 3.5 either way at $\rho = 0.85$ when $k$ is large, and less at small $k$; Appendix~\ref{app:A}). If, in addition, every pair of contributions meets at the same angle, the lost gradient lies between the two measured-profile forms of Appendix~\ref{app:A}, computed from each sequence's measured signal sizes. It does not. On every model with a shrinking signal and every loop count for which we stored those sizes (Huginn at 8--64 loops, Parcae-1.3B at 8 and 16, Parcae-140m at 8--64, Parcae-370m at 16--64 and SmolLM2 (A)), the lost gradient is above both forms, by 0.04--0.18 at $k = 1$ and 2 (difference of medians). On Ouro, whose signal grows, it lies below both measured-profile forms at $k = 1$ (and at the lower of the two geometric forms of Table~\ref{tab:1}'s footnote).

We measured each loop's contribution directly on frozen Parcae-140m and Parcae-370m (16 and 32 loops) and Huginn (32 loops), and the backward signal at 48, 64, 96 and 128 loops (S9, S10, S14). Some of the following was pre-registered and some was read off the same data afterwards; we label which.

\emph{Pre-registered:}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The turning comes from the backward signal (S9).} Near the output, successive loops' contributions point in different directions. On all three models this turning is in the backward signal itself, not in how each loop maps it to the weights (S9, check A).
\item \textbf{$\rho^{k}$ overshoots more as the loop count grows (S10).} From 32 to 64 loops the lost gradient stayed put (Section~\ref{sec:3}) while the fitted $\rho$ rose, from 0.825 to 0.871 on Parcae-140m and from 0.868 to 0.907 on Parcae-370m. At 64 loops, $\rho^{k}$ at $k = 4$ is 1.34 [1.19, 1.47] and 1.46 [1.14, 1.65] times the lost gradient (bootstrap 95\% intervals over the 16 sequences), so the lost gradient falls 25--32\% below $\rho^{k}$ at $k = 4$ and 38--48\% below at $k = 8$. The growth from 32 loops is clear on Parcae-140m (+0.29 [+0.10, +0.35] at $k = 4$) but not resolved on Parcae-370m (+0.20 [$-$0.13, +0.36]), which already overshoots at 32 loops (1.26 [1.03, 1.47]). On Huginn, $\rho$ and the overshoot also rose, while its lost gradient moved just over the pre-set bar (Section~\ref{sec:3}). S10 tested a trend we had already seen at 16--32 loops on Parcae and 16--64 on Huginn; it confirmed the direction, not a quantitative prediction.
\item \textbf{Per token position (S10, descriptive; the reading rule was fixed in advance).} Split by token position, each position's share of the backward signal barely changes from one loop to the next (overlap 0.94--0.99), while the signal's direction at each position does (cosine 0.11--0.44 between the last two loops). This does not separate turning within a position's features from mixing across positions through attention, which could give the same pattern.
\item \textbf{Two checks did not support a single mechanism (S9).} One loop's backward map, applied repeatedly, reproduced the lost gradient and $\rho$ within 0.02 on Huginn but not on either Parcae model (off by 0.032 and 0.020). Whether the turning parts of that map carry both departures described below was uninformative: the map is strongly non-normal.
\end{itemize}

\emph{After the fact, from the same data:}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{What is lost is a sum that shrinks at a steady rate.} The lost gradient is the size of $D_k$, the sum of the dropped loops' contributions. Past the first loop, that sum shrinks by a nearly constant factor per loop on Parcae-140m (0.81--0.84 at $k = 1$--16) and Huginn (0.87--0.89). On Parcae-370m it slows further back (0.82--0.83 at $k = 1$--4, 0.85--0.88 at $k = 6$--16).
\item \textbf{Near the output the sum also turns.} Each contribution is the difference of two successive sums, $c_k = D_k - D_{k+1}$, so where the sum turns, the contribution is larger than the shrinking alone would give: at the last loop 2.5 times on Parcae-140m, 2.7 times on Parcae-370m and 3.8 times on Huginn. Contribution sizes otherwise follow the backward signal (0.9--1.3 times per unit of signal, relative to the last loop), apart from Parcae's first loop, which acts on the random initial state (1.8--1.9).
\item \textbf{One identity covers both departures from the measured-profile forms.} Per-loop sizes fall fast over the first few loops before settling into the sum's rate, and the inflated near-output contributions point sideways, so they add little to the total. An exact identity (Appendix~\ref{app:A}) writes both departures in terms of one quantity, the turning of the tail sum. That is a re-description, not a tested mechanism: the S9 checks built to test a shared source failed on Parcae (B) and were uninformative (C). Why the two departures come out similar in size at these loop counts is not derived.
\item \textbf{A fit over all loops recovers the sum's rate only within a band of loop counts.} The fit is dominated by the loops where the turning has died out, so $\rho^{k}$ lands close. With few loops, the fast near-output fall pulls the fit down and $\rho^{k}$ slightly undershoots: on Parcae-140m and -370m at 16 loops, by up to about 12\% (Parcae-370m at $k = 8$). This does not hold for every model: on Parcae-1.3B at 16 loops $\rho^{k}$ lies 6--10\% above the lost gradient, on SmolLM2 at 8 loops up to 22\% above, and on Huginn at 8 loops the sign is mixed. With many loops, the fit follows the far loops, which fade more slowly than the loops that carry the lost gradient, and $\rho^{k}$ overshoots. S10 tested this last part.
\end{itemize}

\citet[Sec.~5.4]{aicher2019} saw a related far-fade effect in recurrent networks unrolled over time: a few slowly decaying directions of the backward signal pushed their estimated rate close to or above 1. They suggest that if such directions do not influence the parameter gradient they should be ignored, by weighting directions by their effect on the parameters, and leave this to future work. The per-loop contributions do that weighting, and S10's result (the lost gradient stays put while $\rho$ rises) is a measurement in looped transformers consistent with their suggestion. The ingredients of the account are also known: the tail identity (\citealp[App., eq.~9]{liao2018}), a bound set by the slowest mode (\citealp[Prop.~3.1, eq.~10]{shaban2019}), and the observation that truncation diminishes the weight on slow, low-curvature directions (\citealp[Sec.~3.3.1]{vicol2022}).

Section~\ref{sec:3} summarises the blended points (crosses in Figure~\ref{fig:2}; same measurement, same sequences). In more detail: blended Parcae-140m ($R = 32$--64, $\rho$ 0.87--0.94) sits within about 0.03 at every $k$, unlike unblended Parcae at 64 loops; we have no explanation for the difference. Blended SmolLM2 (A) ($R = 8$, $\rho$ 0.62--0.82) falls below it: by 0.10--0.22 at $a = 0.3$ and 0.2, well outside the range of Section~\ref{sec:3} (0.23 measured against $\rho^{4} = 0.45$ at $a = 0.2$), and at $a = 0.5$ by 9--12\% at $k = 1$ and 2, inside that range, but 25\% low at $k = 4$. It even falls below both exact finite-loop forms (Appendix~\ref{app:A}), by 0.02--0.10, so the short loop count does not explain it. Lying below them means that the dropped loops add up less coherently than all loops together (Appendix~\ref{app:A}), or that the contributions do not shrink the way the backward signal does; we did not measure which on this model. For this model under blending, $\rho^{k}$ overestimates the lost gradient.

\section{Ouro's training cost (S18)}\label{app:K}

\textbf{Setup} (frozen before any scored run, and the freeze pushed to GitHub first; Appendix~\ref{app:B}). Ouro-1.4B at a fixed revision, its 24 layers looped 4 times, run in fp32 with fp16 autocast and split across two T4 GPUs. The recipe is Section~\ref{sec:4}'s: GSM8K question-and-answer text in 512-token chunks, 600 steps of batch 8 $\times$ 512, AdamW (0.9, 0.95) with no weight decay, a 20-step warm-up then cosine decay to 10\%, and clipping at 1.0. The loss is the cross-entropy of the last loop's output. As in S11, the token embedding is frozen in every arm: Ouro does not tie it to the output head, so it gets gradient only through the loop, and only full backprop could otherwise train it. Arms: full backprop (all 4 loops, 1.33B trained parameters), the last 2 or last 1 loop, and a baseline with no loop gradient in which only the output head trains (103M; Ouro's final norm sits inside every loop, so it gets no gradient there either). Every job first checked that the split forward pass equals Ouro's own, that checkpointing and the training precision leave the truncated gradient intact, that the baseline gives the loop exactly zero gradient, and that the embedding gets none.

\textbf{Inputs to the predictions.} Ouro's lost gradient was measured in this setting (embedding frozen, 32 GSM8K test chunks): 0.92 at $k = 1$ and 0.84 at $k = 2$, against Table~\ref{tab:1}'s 0.92 and 0.81 over all parameters on 16 validation sequences. The curve, 0.3418 $\cdot$ (lost gradient)$^{3.4335}$, was fitted on lost gradients of 0.18--0.88, so at $k = 1$ it extrapolates.

\textbf{Choosing learning rates on test loss, a deviation from S11.} On a pilot seed, each arm's learning rate was chosen on GSM8K test loss, not on our validation problems (the last 473 of GSM8K's training split, held out from every fine-tune). On Ouro those problems do not behave as unseen text. Before any fine-tuning they are 0.27 nats easier than test (0.891 against 1.162), and under full backprop the validation loss moves opposite to test: it is best at the lowest learning rate tried (3e-6), where test is worst. The likely reason is that Ouro saw GSM8K's training split in its own training. Selecting on validation would have chosen 3e-6, where full backprop does worse on test than the baseline (1.124 against 1.062), so no cost could have been measured. Both full backprop and the baseline (tuned separately, as in S11) chose 1e-4, inside their grids. The scored seeds are new, which does not remove the bias of choosing the rate on the scoring split, and at 40 steps the seed-to-seed spread of full backprop's test loss was 0.0003 nats, far below the gaps between the learning rates compared. Truncated arms use full backprop's rate and were not tuned. (On Parcae and the Part II models, tuning each truncated setting chose full backprop's rate; S2, S19.) Their gradients are smaller, so a higher rate might suit them; the result is a cost at a shared rate, not at each arm's best rate. The baseline alone takes most of the fine-tune's gain on test (0.10 of 0.13 nats on the pilot seed), which is why the loop's own benefit is small.

\textbf{Rules} (fixed in advance). Cost as in Section~\ref{sec:4}, on test, final-output loss; means over seeds with 90\% t-intervals.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Gate:} the baseline's gap must be at least 0.03 nats with its 90\% lower bound above 0, as in S11.
\item \textbf{Seeds:} 3, raised to 5 if the standard error of the $k = 1$ cost over the first 3 exceeded 0.02. It was 0.10, so two more pre-listed seeds ran unchanged. A seed is valid if its full-backprop run has at most 10 unstable steps; all five were valid (at most 3).
\item \textbf{Verdict}, first match: below both predictions at both $k$ (upper bounds below the smaller prediction); above both (lower bounds above the larger); the curve closer (its average error at most half that of Parcae's cost); Parcae's cost closer (the reverse); otherwise a tie.
\end{itemize}
